# Interventions for adolescents with developmental language disorder (DLD): evidence for a self-paced Icelandic trainer

*Research brief for Fallaskóli. Compiled November 2026 from primary sources (journal pages, author-accepted manuscripts, programme and agency pages). Effect sizes are Cohen's d unless stated. "Bespoke" means a researcher-made test of the taught items; "standardised" means a normed test.*

## Executive summary

1. About 7.6% of children have a language disorder of unknown origin (DLD). It persists, and it predicts poor exam results at 16–17 even after IQ is controlled for. Iceland has no prevalence study and almost no SLT provision after compulsory school.
2. For adolescents, the evidence on vocabulary is consistent but narrow. Explicit **semantic + phonological** teaching of chosen words works on *those words* (d ≈ 0.3–1.1). Gains on standardised vocabulary tests are close to zero, and gains fade without revision.
3. People with DLD **encode** new words poorly but **retain** them about as well as peers once they are learned. **Retrieval practice with spacing** beats restudy, and the benefit is if anything larger for DLD. No adolescent trial of this exists; it is bracketed by trials with 4–6-year-olds and with adults.
4. For sentence comprehension, the strongest adolescent evidence is **explicit, visual, metalinguistic** grammar teaching (Shape Coding): about 4 hours gave d ≈ 1.3–1.6 on taught structures, with limited transfer to untaught ones.
5. Complex-sentence production (adverbial, complement and relative clauses) improved in 10–14-year-olds, but the gains did not reach reading or writing.
6. Inference and summarising instruction helps struggling readers in grades 3–12 (summarising: g ≈ 0.97), but those samples are not DLD samples. An oral-language programme improved comprehension through vocabulary gains.
7. Narrative intervention has the most consistent evidence of the three goals. Meta-analyses give d ≈ 0.5 (group designs) and about 1.2 (single-case designs). Effects are larger on **macrostructure** (story grammar) than on microstructure. Story-grammar icons, modelling and telling a whole story every session are the recurring active ingredients.
8. The one secondary-school RCT (Joffe 2019, ages 11–13, delivered by teaching assistants) moved standardised narrative scores modestly (d ≈ 0.30) and did not move standardised vocabulary.
9. Stand-alone computer programs have a poor record: Fast ForWord had pooled effects of about zero. Digital delivery of *explicit, targeted* content looks as good as face-to-face for taught words, but nearly all the evidence is from children under 12.
10. For Fallaskóli the best-supported moves are: few curriculum-relevant words taught deeply (meaning + form + use), self-testing with spaced review, explicit visual sentence-structure coding, and story-grammar scaffolds with fading. Oral narrative coaching, and judging free production, are where software is weakest.

## A. DLD in adolescence

- **Terminology.** CATALISE's Delphi consensus uses *language disorder* for language problems that impair daily life and carry a poor prognosis. *DLD* is the term when there is no known biomedical cause. A gap between verbal and nonverbal ability is not required, and DLD can co-occur with conditions such as ADHD [Bishop et al. 2017](https://open.bu.edu/items/dddf0e0d-8ed5-476f-be44-c54fd5b8a31b). The Icelandic term *málþroskaröskun DLD* dates from 2017 [Málefli / Félag talmeinafræðinga 2021](https://www.malefli.is/static/files/fraedsla/dld-stadreyndablad-2021.pdf).
- **Prevalence.** A population study of 7,267 English school entrants estimated 7.58% (95% CI 5.33–10.66) with language disorder of unknown origin. Most did not make expected academic progress [Norbury et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5082564).
- **Persistence and school outcomes.** In a UK cohort of 120 adolescents with a history of SLI, only 44% gained at least one expected qualification, and nearly a quarter sat no national exams. Language and literacy predicted attainment beyond IQ and maternal education. The authors single out the move to secondary school as a key point for support [Conti-Ramsden et al. 2009](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/19928/).
- **Vocabulary is a lasting weak point.** Adults with DLD at university still learn new word forms less well, and they need more exposures to reach mastery [McGregor et al. 2020](https://doaj.org/article/7293fce2e16040159e0bec20c764dd05).

## B. Vocabulary interventions

### Reviews

- **Lowe, Henry, Müller & Joffe (2018), systematic review, ages 11–16.** Of 1,320 records, 13 studies qualified: 4 semantic, 2 semantic-vs-phonological comparisons, 7 combined phonological–semantic. The combined approach had the strongest evidence. The authors call that evidence tentative but emerging, with individual, small-group and whole-class delivery all possibly useful [Lowe et al. 2018](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/18543/); [ASHA Evidence Map summary](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/9b5a90a3-9818-44b1-8bf2-9c5c71add33e).
- **Ansari et al. (2025), systematic review, ages 5–11 (16 studies, 288 children).** Explicit phonological + semantic teaching had the most high-quality positive studies. Stories helped as added context but did less on their own. Gains were limited to taught words and often declined after therapy. Children with more severe difficulties used context cues less well [Ansari et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11962024).

**Takeaway.** Taught words are learned (moderate-to-large effects). Standardised vocabulary does not move in 6 weeks. Gains erode without revision, which is why both Lowe 2019 and Wright 2018 recommend built-in review.

### Encoding, retrieval and spacing (mechanism studies)

- **Retrieval beats restudy.** In 5-year-olds with DLD, repeated spaced retrieval with a 0–3–3 schedule beat repeated study for recalling word forms at 5 minutes and at 1 week (d ≈ 0.95–1.09) [Leonard et al. 2019](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2019/2019_Leonard_et_al_JSLHR_Part_1.pdf). A pooled analysis of four studies (53 DLD, 52 TD, ages 4–5) found a retrieval advantage that was slightly larger for DLD, and largest for word *forms*, their weakest area. Retention from 5 minutes to 1 week matched peers [Leonard et al. 2021](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2021/2021_Leonard_et_al_JNDD.pdf).
- **Encoding is the bottleneck.** In postsecondary students with DLD, the deficit was in encoding, not in retention. Self-testing with feedback reduced the gap, and passive study helped them least [McGregor et al. 2017](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Encoding-Deficits-Impede-Word-Learning-and/9983997361802771).
- **More exposures and well-timed review.** Adults with DLD needed more retrieval trials to reach mastery and made more word-form errors. Retrieval review at 1 day and at 1 week improved 1-month retention, and the 1-week review helped more [McGregor et al. 2020](https://doaj.org/article/7293fce2e16040159e0bec20c764dd05).
- *Gap:* no retrieval-practice word-learning study with 13–16-year-olds with DLD was found. The inference to teens is interpolation between preschool and adult data (moderate confidence).

### Morphology and figurative vocabulary

- **Morphological instruction.** A meta-analysis of 17 studies of children with literacy difficulties found an overall effect of d = 0.33, a vocabulary effect of d = 0.40, and the largest subgroup effect for children with speech/language delays (d = 0.77). Effects appeared only with at least 10 hours and when morphology was embedded in broader instruction [Goodwin & Ahn 2010](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/93cb3843-8a27-4b52-887b-32781be13e92). The studies were in English, so applying this to Icelandic, which is morphologically richer, is plausible but untested.
- **Idioms.** With 49 pupils with DLD aged 9–16, 20 × 30-min sessions improved explanation and use of taught idioms. One-to-one SLT delivery beat classroom delivery. Gains held at 3 months, but there was no convincing transfer to untaught idioms [Benjamin, Newton & Ebbels 2020](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10088872/1/Benjamin%2C%20Newton%20%26%20Ebbels%20%28in%20press%29.pdf). In typical preadolescents, idiom understanding tracked familiarity and reading/listening comprehension [Nippold et al. 2001](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/1058-0360(2001/016)).

## C. Sentence comprehension, inference, figurative language, summarising

### Grammar and syntax

- **Review.** Explicit, metalinguistic approaches are the ones with evidence for school-aged and secondary pupils, including those with receptive difficulties. Implicit recasting is mainly supported for younger, expressive-only children. Transfer to untrained structures is inconsistent [Ebbels 2014](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1469502/1/Child%20Language%20Teaching%20and%20Therapy-2014-Ebbels-7-40.pdf).
- **Shape Coding, comprehension RCT (ages 11–16, severe receptive DLD, N = 14).** Eight 30-minute sessions (about 4 h) targeted *but not*, *neither…nor* and *not only…but also*. Results: d = 1.64 against waiting controls on the targets; combined d = 1.33; gains held at 4 months. TROG-2 improved, but passives (the control structure) did not [Ebbels et al. 2014](https://core.ac.uk/download/110962093.pdf).
- **Shape Coding, verb argument structure RCT (ages 11–16, N = 27).** About 4.5 hours of syntactic-semantic or semantic-only verb therapy beat a control arm (d > 1.0). Gains reached some untaught verbs and were reduced but still significant at 3 months [Ebbels, van der Lely & Dockrell 2007](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10111888/1/Ebbels%20et%20al%202007%20arg%20struc%20therapy%20%28accepted%29.pdf).
- **Complex sentences (ages 10–14, N = 30).** Adverbial, then object complement, then relative clauses, taught in sequence. Most participants improved on sentence probes, with the largest effects for adverbial and relative clauses, and there were clinically meaningful gains on an oral language test. Twice-weekly sessions did not beat once-weekly. Reading and writing did not improve [Balthazar & Scott 2018](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2017_JSLHR-L-17-0105).

### Inference, reading comprehension, summarising

- **Oral-language route to comprehension.** In an RCT with poor comprehenders (primary age), text-comprehension, oral-language and combined programmes all improved reading comprehension. The oral-language group kept gaining after the programme ended, and its gains were partly mediated by expressive vocabulary [Clarke et al. 2010](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/0956797610375449/).
- **Inference instruction.** A synthesis of 9 studies (grades 2–9) found g = 0.72–1.85 on researcher-made inference measures but mixed results on standardised tests. Teaching clue-word use was the most common effective method, and simply giving many inferential questions after reading may work about as well [Hall 2016](https://meadowscenter.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/InferenceInstruction1.pdf).
- **Summarising and main idea.** A review of 30 studies of struggling readers in grades 3–12 found a group-design effect of 0.97 on reading comprehension, with no difference by grade, group size or number of sessions [Stevens et al. 2019](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/24580a55-4e21-4b20-a910-b8fda606eccd). *Caveat:* these were struggling readers, not diagnosed DLD.
- **Summarising rules (Brown & Day 1983).** The "macrorules" behind most summary instruction since: **delete** what is trivial and what is said twice; **superordinate**, putting one word for a list of things or actions (*fuglar* for *lundar, kríur og mávar*); **select** the topic sentence when the text has one; **invent** one when it does not. Across three studies, pupils in grades 5 and 7 mostly used a "copy-delete" strategy (keep sentences, drop some), while older high-school and college students also combined information and wrote topic sentences in their own words; invention was the last rule to develop, and junior-college students performed like typical seventh graders [Brown & Day 1983](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(83)80002-4). In the trainer (issue #32) the rules become three steps and three kinds of wrong summary: *select* is "which sentence is the main idea" (`les_setning`, with the main idea not always first), *delete* is "which sentences support it" (`les_smaatridi`, with one sentence about something else to leave out), and the best-gist question offers one summary that is **too narrow** (a single detail, the copy-delete error), one **too broad** (a superordinate so general it fits any text) and one that **contradicts or invents** (`les_kjarni`), each explained after a wrong pick. *Caveat:* the study describes typically developing students and is about how the skill develops, not an intervention trial; the order of the rules, not an effect size, is what is taken from it.
- **Figurative language.** The idiom trial above is the only adolescent DLD trial found, and its results were item-specific (Section B).

## D. Narrative intervention

- **Meta-analyses.** Pico et al. found 40 school-age studies, with meta-analytic effects of d ≈ 0.51–0.54 (group designs) and 1.24 (single-case designs). Common features: manualised curricula, frequent chances to tell stories, verbal *and visual* supports, direct teaching of story grammar, and real children's literature [Pico et al. 2021](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2021_LSHSS-20-00160). Favot, Carter & Stephenson (24 studies, children with language disorder): single-case effects were moderate for **macrostructure** and lower for **microstructure**, and group studies were weak and inconsistent. Successful programmes used icons, visuals, modelling, and a full story told by the child every session [Favot et al. 2021](https://vufind-test.katalog.k.utb.cz/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c319t-138c01ca933ed9906ed9c6da02a681595dcf5ffab6c0e33a295bfa1ccd342f113).
- **Secondary school.** In Joffe 2019, the narrative arm (Stein & Glenn story grammar, story types and genres) improved the standardised narrative factor (pooled d = 0.30) and a bespoke story-generation task (d = 0.95). The vocabulary arm did not change narrative [Joffe et al. 2019](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/21984/1/IJLCD_2019_Joffe%20et%20al.pdf).
- **SKILL (Gillam & Gillam).** 36 × 30-minute lessons in small groups, run by teachers, in three phases: story structure and causal language, situation-model strategies, then memory strategies. The techniques are *parallel story production* (model, then co-construct a similar story) and *vertical structuring* (combining short sentences into complex ones). In an RCT with 357 pupils in grades 1–4, SKILL beat business-as-usual on oral narrative at post-test and at 5 months, transferred to written narration, and narrative ability mediated the gains in reading comprehension [IES award summary](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/awards/randomized-controlled-trial-supporting-knowledge-language-and-literacy-skill-program-children-who); [Gillam et al. 2023](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED622886).
- **Literate narrative intervention.** In a small early-stage study with children aged 6–8, 10 sessions targeting story grammar *and* causal language improved both. Some untargeted microstructure also rose, and some gains held at 8 months [Petersen et al. 2010](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/1092-4388(2009/09-0001)).
- **Icons and structured scripts.** With children with ASD + language disorder aged 9–10, five elements (character, setting, problem, feelings, fix) were taught with macrostructure icons and modelling. Three of four children improved, held the gains, and transferred them to fantasy stories [Favot et al. 2022](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/the-effects-of-an-oral-narrative-intervention-on-the-fictional-na-2/).
- **Retell vs generation.** None of the meta-analyses separates the two. Programmes generally move from retelling a model to generating a parallel story (SKILL, Joffe's generation task), so "retell before generate" is standard practice, not a tested comparison.
- **Grammar inside narrative.** A narrative-plus-grammar package improved narrative quality but not grammar, as cited in [Ebbels 2014](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1469502/1/Child%20Language%20Teaching%20and%20Therapy-2014-Ebbels-7-40.pdf). Microstructure needs explicit targeting.

## E. Digital and self-paced delivery

- **Fast ForWord (acoustically modified speech, game-based).** A meta-analysis of 5 RCTs found no reliable effect on any outcome; receptive language d = 0.01 and expressive d = −0.04 against untreated controls [Strong et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3061204). In a 216-child RCT (ages 6–9), Fast ForWord was no better than computer-assisted language intervention, SLT-delivered intervention, *or academic enrichment*. All groups improved, which points to general engagement and dose rather than the acoustic manipulation [Gillam et al. 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18230858/).
- **Systematic review of digital DLD interventions.** 13 studies (961 children, mean ages 3–11; none adolescent). Vocabulary and basic phonological training looked promising. Grammar gains often did not reach standardised receptive grammar tests. Fidelity was low in 9 of 13 studies. The authors see digital tools as an add-on to in-person therapy [Zhou et al. 2025](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2025/1/e59992/PDF).
- **Tablet vs real objects.** In a non-inferiority RCT with 3-year-olds with DLD, tablet-game vocabulary teaching was as effective as real objects (SLP-mediated) [Zwitserlood et al. 2022](https://mdpi.com/2076-3417/12/3/1643).
- **Self-paced practice was never tested for DLD teens.** But the same *ingredients* that work face-to-face are computer-friendly: retrieval with feedback (McGregor 2017), explicit visual coding (Ebbels 2014), spaced review (McGregor 2020). The Icelandic evidence also warns that teacher-delivered academic vocabulary teaching without enough intensity showed no group effect in 10th grade [Ólafsdóttir et al. 2020](https://opinvisindi.is/items/2b9e0f9d-2444-416d-888b-0e8797d87f67).
- **Honest summary.** There is no RCT of any self-paced app for adolescents with DLD. The evidence against "brain-training" style generic programs is strong. The evidence for digitally delivered *targeted, explicit* content is weak to moderate.

## F. Icelandic context and resources

- **Services.** A 2012 report to the Ministry of Education found no epidemiology, about 400 children on private SLT waiting lists, no single responsible institution for severe language disorder since 2006, and **no SLT in upper secondary schools**. Youth aged 16–18 must find services themselves [Hrafnhildur Ragnarsdóttir et al. 2012](https://www.stjornarradid.is/media/menntamalaraduneyti-media/media/ritogskyrslur/skyrsl_stodu_barna_ungm_malthroskarosk_2012.pdf). The report applies international rates to project roughly 350–565 affected children per birth cohort.
- **Tests named in that report:** Orðaskil (18–36 months), TOLD-P:2 and TOLD-I:2 (4–13), HLJÓM-2, Framburðarpróf (Sigríður Magnúsdóttir & Höskuldur Þráinsson), Málhljóðaskimun (Þóra Másdóttir), EFI-2, TRAS [same source](https://www.stjornarradid.is/media/menntamalaraduneyti-media/media/ritogskyrslur/skyrsl_stodu_barna_ungm_malthroskarosk_2012.pdf). *No Icelandic-normed language test covering ages 13–16 was confirmed*; a CELF Icelandic adaptation was not verified.
- **Narrative assessment.** MAIN (Multilingual Assessment Instrument for Narratives) was adapted to Icelandic by Hrafnhildur Ragnarsdóttir. It scores story structure (setting, goal, attempt, outcome, reaction), inner-state terms and comprehension questions, and its target age is about 3–10, with use also at older ages [Ragnarsdóttir 2020](https://www.leibniz-zas.de/fileadmin/media/Dokumente/ZASPiL/ZASPiL_63/MAIN_Icelandic_2020_Revised.pdf). Icelandic later-language development (ages 11, 14, 17 and adults) has been studied across narrative vs expository and spoken vs written texts [Ragnarsdóttir 2011](https://timarit.is/page/5610684?lang=en).
- **Icelandic DLD grammar.** Icelandic children with SLI made verb and noun-phrase inflection errors at similar rates. Overall error rates were lower than in English, and inflection accuracy did not vary with context the way it did in English [Thordardottir 2008](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/1092-4388%282008/068%29). This suggests Icelandic DLD may show less in inflection and more in vocabulary, syntax and discourse, which bears on how much inflection drilling helps the DLD goal.
- **Academic vocabulary list (key resource).** **LÍNO-2**: 2,294 Tier-2 lemmas in five frequency bands, drawn from a 31.7-million-word school-text corpus (MÍNO, built from Risamálheild + MÍM, including Menntamálastofnun teaching materials) [Pálsdóttir & Ólafsdóttir 2023](https://opinvisindi.is/items/5637c58b-05cd-498d-8ea0-0734776a07fe). This is the natural target list for goal 1.
- **Icelandic adolescent vocabulary trial.** 10th-grade academic-vocabulary professional development for teachers: no group difference (157 vs 88 pupils), with non-significant positive trends in comprehension [Ólafsdóttir et al. 2020](https://opinvisindi.is/items/2b9e0f9d-2444-416d-888b-0e8797d87f67).
- **Other resources.** *Orðaforðalisti* (Menntamálastofnun 2017) is a preschool concept list, not an assessment [Elsa Pálsdóttir 2017](https://mms.is/sites/mms.is/files/ordalisti.pdf). The Icelandic Gigaword Corpus (Risamálheild) has about 1.3 billion words, lemmatised and PoS-tagged automatically (not hand-corrected), partly under CC BY 4.0 [CLARIN-IS](https://clarin.is/en/resources/gigaword); [Steingrímsson et al. 2018](https://aclanthology.org/L18-1690/). *Lesferill* (Menntamálastofnun) includes a sight-vocabulary test with a grade 8–10 version [MMS 2021](https://mms.is/sites/mms.is/files/tulkun_studningsprofa_2._utg_2021.pdf). It is now succeeded by *Matsferill*, with mandatory reading-comprehension tests in grades 4, 6 and 9 and school-level results published from 2027 [Stjórnarráðið 2026](https://www.stjornarradid.is/frettir/grein/2026-03-04-Birting-a-namsframvindu-barna-i-lesskilningi-staerdfraedi-og-lesfimi/).
- **Not found / not verified:** "Orðalykill", an Icelandic adolescent narrative test, Icelandic DLD intervention trials of any kind, and published work by Jóhanna Thelma Einarsdóttir or Elín Þöll Þórðardóttir on *adolescent* narrative or vocabulary intervention.

## G. Translation into a self-paced trainer

Strength key: **Strong** = several controlled studies in the target group or a meta-analysis; **Moderate** = consistent smaller trials, or a meta-analysis in an adjacent group; **Weak** = single small or uncontrolled studies; **Extrapolated** = from mechanism or adjacent populations only.

### Goal 1: functional and academic vocabulary

1. **Teach a small, chosen set of words deeply instead of many shallowly**: about 10–30 per block, from LÍNO-2 bands 1–2 plus the curriculum. Gains are word-specific, so choose the words that matter (Lowe 2018/2019; Spencer 2017; Ansari 2025). **Moderate.**
2. **Join meaning and form on every word**: definition, category, function, synonym/antonym, *plus* syllables, first sound, rhyme, and in Icelandic stem and inflection. TTS supplies the spoken form, BÍN the paradigm (Lowe 2018; Wright 2018; Leonard 2021: form is the weakest link). **Moderate.**
3. **Retrieval with feedback, not re-reading.** Make the pupil *produce* the word or its meaning (free recall, cloze, then choice) before showing the answer. Avoid passive "flashcard flip" study (McGregor 2017; Leonard 2019/2021). **Moderate** for the mechanism; **extrapolated** to teens.
4. **Expanding spaced review that continues after mastery.** Include roughly 1-day and 1-week reviews early, then longer ones. Without review, gains erode by follow-up (McGregor 2020; Lowe 2019; Wright 2018). The current 1→3→7→21 queue fits; consider allowing *same-session* spaced retrieval (0–3–3 style) on first learning, which the "never same day" rule blocks. **Moderate.**
5. **Plan for more exposures**, with a mastery criterion rather than a fixed count (McGregor 2020). **Moderate.**
6. **Use the word in several sentence contexts and in the pupil's own sentence.** Ritun vikunnar can require a target word (Spencer 2017; Wright 2018). **Weak–moderate.**
7. **Word formation and morphology**, embedded rather than isolated, and needing more than 10 hours in total (Goodwin & Ahn 2010). **Moderate** in literacy-difficulty groups; **extrapolated** to Icelandic.
8. **Idioms and figurative meaning as taught items** (explain + use), with no expectation of transfer to untaught idioms (Benjamin 2020). **Weak–moderate.**

### Goal 2: understanding complex sentences and texts

1. **Explicit, visual coding of sentence structure.** Colour/shape-coded phrase roles, clause boundaries and connectives (*ekki aðeins…heldur einnig*, *hvorki…né*). A Greynir parse can generate the coding automatically, which fits "checkable" content (Ebbels 2007/2014). **Moderate** (small RCTs, large effects, specialist-school samples).
2. **Teach one structure at a time, in sequence**: adverbial → complement → relative clauses. Present new structures as they come up, and do not expect transfer (Balthazar & Scott 2018; Ebbels 2014). **Weak–moderate.**
3. **Sentence-combining ("vertical structuring") and sentence-decomposing tasks**: two simple sentences ↔ one complex sentence, checked against the parse (SKILL; Balthazar & Scott). **Weak–moderate.**
4. **Many short inference questions with clue-word highlighting** after short texts (Lesefni): "Which words told you?" (Hall 2016). **Moderate** (struggling readers, not DLD).
5. **Main-idea and summary practice**, as multiple-choice "best summary" and "which sentence is the main idea" items first, then constrained written summaries (Stevens 2019), with wrong summaries built on Brown & Day's (1983) rules: too narrow, too broad, or wrong. **Moderate** (struggling readers).
6. **Build vocabulary in support of comprehension.** In Clarke 2010, comprehension gains ran through vocabulary, so goals 1 and 2 should share words and texts. **Moderate.**

### Goal 3: structured, coherent stories

1. **Story grammar icons** (persóna, staður, vandi, tilfinning, tilraun, lausn/endir) shown on screen as a template and attached to sentences (Favot 2021/2022; Pico 2021; Joffe 2019). **Moderate–strong** for macrostructure.
2. **Model → retell → parallel story → own story, with fading**: start with a fully iconised model story, have the pupil reorder or sort sentences into slots, then fill a slot frame, then write a parallel story with fewer prompts (SKILL; Petersen 2010; Pico 2021). **Moderate**; the sequencing itself is **extrapolated** practice.
3. **A complete story every session**, short is fine (Favot 2021). **Moderate.**
4. **Explicit cohesion and causal-language targets**: *af því að*, *þess vegna*, *þegar*, *en*, and reference with pronouns. These must be taught directly, because macrostructure work does not carry microstructure along (Petersen 2010; Favot 2021; Ebbels 2014). **Weak–moderate.**
5. **Checkable story tasks**: which story-grammar element is missing, order the scrambled episode, choose the connective. Use these rather than auto-grading free stories. **Extrapolated** (design constraint).

### What software cannot replicate, and mitigations

| Missing ingredient | Why it matters | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Live clinician modelling and oral co-construction | Core to SKILL, Story Champs-type and literate narrative programmes | Teacher/TA "story session" mode: the app shows the icons and the model, and the adult runs the oral retell. Joffe 2019 shows TA delivery is feasible. |
| Feedback on oral narratives | Most narrative gains are measured orally | Optional on-device audio recording with a self-check against the icon checklist; teacher listens via the dashboard. No speech recognition is needed. |
| Judging free written production | Wrong feedback is worse than none | Restrict automatic scoring to structural checks (slots filled, connectives present, GreynirCorrect for grammar only). Content judgement goes to the teacher. |
| Individual tailoring of word sets | Profile predicts response (Ansari 2025) | Teacher-selectable word sets; diagnostic pretest per block. |
| Transfer to class | Effects are mostly item-specific | Align word sets and texts with current subjects; give teachers a "this week's words" sheet (Lowe 2019's key-word sheets). |
| Motivation without adult contact | Self-paced dropout | Existing XP/badges, plus short sessions and visible "words I now know" progress (self-rating was part of Word Discovery). |

## Open questions and gaps

- No RCT of any self-paced or app-delivered intervention for **adolescents** with DLD; no trial at all of DLD intervention **in Icelandic**.
- Retrieval-practice and spacing research in DLD covers preschoolers and adults; the optimal schedule for teens is unknown.
- Standardised vocabulary rarely moves in short trials. Whether long-term, cumulative self-paced practice changes general vocabulary or reading is untested.
- Shape Coding evidence comes from one specialist school with severe DLD and one-to-one SLT delivery. Self-delivered visual coding is untested.
- Retell-before-generate, fading schedules and the narrative dose for teenagers have not been compared experimentally.
- Whether Icelandic inflection drills help DLD-relevant outcomes is unclear. Thordardottir 2008 suggests inflection is relatively less affected in Icelandic.
- No Icelandic norm-referenced language or narrative test covering ages 13–16 was found, which makes outcome measurement hard.
- Word Aware (Parsons & Branagan) is widely used, but no controlled trial was found; the closest is Word Discovery (Lowe 2019).
